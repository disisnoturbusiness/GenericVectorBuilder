Seventeen engines on the same 524 vectors and 20 questions: speed, load and recall

Writeups Benchmarks ← Back to search

Seventeen engines, nineteen configurations, the same 524 vectors and the same 20 questions. With 8 searchers, MariaDB answered the most searches per second in all three runs, and pgvector came second in all three. Redis was the fastest with one searcher and 7th with eight. The two in-process engines, DuckDB and sqlite-vec, answered fewer searches with eight searchers than with one. Sixteen configurations returned the exact top 10 on every question in every run.

Setup

  • Box: Intel Xeon E5-1620 v3 at 3.50 GHz, 8 logical CPUs, 62.7 GiB RAM, NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3060 12 GB, Ubuntu 24.04.5. The benchmark client (.NET 10) and every engine ran on this one box.
  • One engine at a time. Docker engines were started for their turn and stopped after it. SQL Server and Qdrant are always-on services on the box. DuckDB and sqlite-vec run inside the client process.
  • Data: eShopOnWeb at commit 4da8212117e8. 254 C# files, 524 chunks, 1,024-dimension vectors from qwen3-emb-0.6b, built by GenericVectorBuilder. Every engine got the same vectors.
  • Queries: the 20 hand-labelled golden questions, embedded once and cached. Every engine got the same query vectors. Top 10.
  • Per engine, in this order: empty collection, load, and a wait until the engine counts 524 rows. Then 20 warm-up searches and 200 timed single searches. Then 20 seconds with 1 searcher and 20 seconds with 8 concurrent searchers. Last, the engine's exact mode if it has one (20 searches). SQL DiskANN builds its index, and Qdrant waits for green status, before the warm-up.
  • Scoring: recall@10 against a brute-force search over the same vectors in memory, with exact ties counted. nDCG@10 against the labels, by file. Both come from the first pass of the 20 questions.
  • Runs: three, on 3 Oct 18:44 UTC and 4 Oct 13:08 and 13:27 UTC. SQL DiskANN joined at run 2. Its third run is a four-target run from 3 Oct 23:48 UTC. Tables show the median of three runs. No search returned an error.
  • Box load: the load average when searching began ranged from 1.8 to 8.3 on 8 threads. The harness flagged two passes as busy: Vespa in run 1, DuckDB in run 3. Both are kept.

Two other settings trade durability too. MariaDB flushed its log once a second, so a crash can lose the last second of writes. DuckDB's index ran with DuckDB's experimental persistence switch, which DuckDB documents as not fully crash safe.

Answers: the same, with three exceptions

Brute force over these vectors scores nDCG@10 0.518 on the labels. Sixteen configurations returned exactly that top 10 for every question in every run. Three did not:

  • SQL Server DiskANN: missed 7 of 200 top-10 slots in every run. Recall 0.965, nDCG 0.526.
  • ClickHouse (bf16 graph, no rescoring): missed 3 of 200 in run 1 and 1 of 200 in runs 2 and 3. Recall 0.985 and 0.995, nDCG 0.520 and 0.528. Its full-precision exact mode returned recall 1.000 in every run.
  • MongoDB: missed 7 of 200 in run 3 only. Recall 0.965, nDCG 0.535. Runs 1 and 2 were exact.

In all three, nDCG went up, not down. On 20 questions a few swapped chunks can move it either way.

Why the order came out this way

Each cause is tagged. measured means a number in the results shows it. likely means it fits the numbers and the client code, but was not isolated.

Pass order moved the numbers measured

The same query, timed later, came back faster:

  • sqlite-vec's exact mode is the same query as its default search. Timed last, its p50 was 1.95 ms against 2.16 ms in the first timed pass. Lower in all three runs.
  • Qdrant with exact=true: 2.30 ms p50 as its own target, 0.87 ms when the same search was timed at the end of the next target. Lower in all three runs.
  • SQL Server's exact scan: 6.18 to 6.81 ms p50 as the first target of a run. The same query, timed inside the DiskANN target that followed, took 4.28 to 4.77 ms.
  • Elasticsearch: one searcher completed 148 to 167 searches a second, 6.0 to 6.8 ms each. The timed pass just before it had a p50 of 11.0 to 13.2 ms. OpenSearch: 6.7 to 7.7 ms against 9.8 to 12.3 ms.

So 20 warm-up searches were not enough for every engine, or for the client. Which side was still warming up was not separated. The p50 column carries part of it, Elasticsearch and OpenSearch the most. The rates come from 20-second passes and are less exposed. The ranking uses the 8-searcher pass, which ran after the timed single searches and the 1-searcher pass.

Five configurations got more than 8 times their 1-searcher rate with 8 searchers, in every run: Elasticsearch (15.2 to 17.8), OpenSearch (11.3 to 14.4), Milvus (10.8 to 11.4), MongoDB (9.1 to 11.6) and MariaDB (8.2 to 10.5). On 8 threads, a gain above 8 means a lone search spent part of its time waiting or warming up.

In-process engines ran one search at a time measured

DuckDB and sqlite-vec each hold one connection per collection and let one call through at a time (client code). With 8 searchers they finished fewer searches than with one, in every run: DuckDB 0.67 to 0.78 times its 1-searcher rate, sqlite-vec 0.87 to 0.97. Alone, sqlite-vec was 2nd of 19 at 527 a second. With eight it was 18th.

Low gains with 8 searchers

  • likely Oracle: 1.8 to 2.7 times its 1-searcher rate. Oracle caps the Free edition at 2 CPU threads. Not tested on a licensed edition.
  • likely Redis: 2.6 to 3.9 times. All 8 searchers share one multiplexed client connection (StackExchange.Redis). Whether the client or the server is the limit was not tested. Run 3 reached 3,154 a second against 2,011 and 2,107 in runs 1 and 2. That spread is not explained.
  • Not explained by this data: Chroma (2.0 to 3.9 times), ClickHouse (4.0 to 4.3) and both SQL Server rows (4.0 to 4.8). Chroma managed 331 a second with 8 searchers in run 1, against 600 and 610 in the others.

How the query vector travels likely

The 1-searcher rates sort into bands by how the client sends the 1,024 floats:

  • Binary (float32 bytes or a native vector type): Redis, MariaDB, Qdrant, pgvector, Oracle, MongoDB. 253 to 783 a second.
  • A JSON array in an HTTP body: Vespa, Chroma, Elasticsearch, Milvus, OpenSearch. 145 to 201 a second.
  • Decimal text inside a query string: ClickHouse (SQL over HTTP), Typesense (the vector_query string in a JSON POST), Weaviate (GraphQL). 64 to 167 a second.
  • SQL Server sends JSON text in a TDS parameter and casts it to VECTOR(1024): 192 a second exact, 227 with DiskANN.

This is at most part of the story. The format was never varied with the engine held fixed. The bands touch: ClickHouse (167) is level with Chroma (168). And the same JSON-over-HTTP clients got exact-mode answers at 2.22 ms p50 from Vespa and 4.29 to 4.41 ms from OpenSearch and Elasticsearch. For those three, the format costs at most that much per search.

Milvus: Strong consistency likely

Every Milvus search ran at Strong consistency, the client's default. Milvus gained 10.8 to 11.4 times with 8 searchers, the steadiest gain above 8 in the set. Its lone searches were waiting on something. Strong consistency is the likely wait. Other levels were not tested, and warm-up may add to it.

Weaviate: GraphQL text likely

Weaviate had the slowest single search, 15.36 ms p50, and the lowest 1-searcher rate, 64 a second. The client writes the 1,024 floats as decimal text into a GraphQL query, which the server parses on every search. Weaviate's gRPC port was open and not used. Weaviate has no exact mode, so there is no same-path baseline to check against.

Index choices measured

  • Qdrant built no graph. After each load it reported 0 of 524 vectors in HNSW segments. Both Qdrant rows are scans, one with exact=true and one with exact=false. Their 8-searcher ranges overlap: 3,051 to 3,444 and 3,041 to 3,432.
  • ClickHouse's bf16 graph without rescoring lost recall in every run. Its exact mode did not.
  • MariaDB searched with mhnsw_ef_search 3,200, six times the row count. pgvector forced the index path with enable_seqscan off. Both kept recall 1.000.

SQL Server: exact scan against DiskANN measuredcause likely

Paired within each run, DiskANN served more searches than the exact scan: 1.5%, 12.2% and 16.4% more with 8 searchers, 14.7%, 18.4% and 15.3% more with one. The exact target always ran first and DiskANN second. Inside the DiskANN target, the exact query timed later ran at 4.28 to 4.77 ms p50, level with DiskANN's 4.24 to 4.56 ms. So the gap between the two SQL rows is likely run order, not the index.

At 524 rows, DiskANN bought no measured speed. It cost recall (0.965). On SQL Server for Linux it is a preview feature that has to be switched on per database, and it makes the table read-only. It also needs a single INT clustered key, so the rows were copied into a second, INT-keyed table before the index build.

What this does not show

  • Scale. 524 vectors. Qdrant built no graph at all. Nothing here predicts behaviour at 100,000 or 10 million vectors.
  • Where the time goes. At this size, per-call cost (client, protocol, request handling) likely outweighs the search itself. Likely, not measured. No empty-call baseline was run, and the exact-mode timings that hint at it ran last.
  • A clean client. The client shared the same 8 threads with each engine. With 8 searchers, the client's own work competes with the engine. That was not separated.
  • Concurrency curves. One level above 1. Nothing about 16 or 64 searchers, or where each engine tops out.
  • Index readiness for every engine. The harness waits for the row count, the DiskANN build and Qdrant's green status. It does not wait for Milvus's background index build or for Oracle to merge new rows into its graph. Some of their searches may have run without the full graph. Not checked.
  • Tuning. One setting per engine, mostly M 16 and a search beam of 100. No sweep on this data.
  • Memory and disk. Recorded, and left out. RAM is the whole SQL Server or Qdrant process, with everything else they serve. For containers it is docker stats. For the two in-process engines it was not measured. Disk ranges from one table's pages to a whole data folder. The numbers do not compare across engines.
  • Bulk loading. One writer and 524 rows. The Load column is not a bulk-load test.

Settings, per engine

Load rows/s counts time inside each engine's write calls only: one writer, rows already in memory, into an empty collection. The harness hands over all 524 rows in one call. Each client then splits them:

  • 1 batch: SQL Server (bulk copy, then MERGE), ClickHouse, DuckDB, sqlite-vec.
  • 2 batches of up to 500: Redis, MongoDB, Oracle, MariaDB, pgvector, Elasticsearch, OpenSearch, Typesense, Chroma, Milvus.
  • 3 batches: Qdrant (256 each), Weaviate (200 each).
  • Vespa: one document per request, up to 64 in flight.

Work after the writes is not in the number. SQL DiskANN's copy and index build took 3.66 to 3.73 s. Milvus took 1.57 to 1.64 s, and MongoDB 0.70 to 1.39 s, before they counted all 524 rows. The two Qdrant targets write the same way. The second, timed right after the first, loaded 1.8 to 2.8 times faster in every run. Run order likely shows here too.

Reproduce

The tool is the bench program in GenericVectorBuilder. It is not public yet. Runs 2 and 3 were this one command. Run 1 left out sql-diskann.

GenericVectorBuilder.Bench run-all --pipeline eshoponweb --targets sql,sql-diskann,qdrant,qdrant-hnsw,pgvector,mariadb,oracle,redis,mongodb,clickhouse,milvus,weaviate,chroma,elasticsearch,opensearch,typesense,vespa,duckdb,sqlitevec --queries golden

Everything else is the default: concurrency 1 and 8, 20 seconds per level, 20 warm-up searches, top 10, up to 60 seconds of exact mode. Each run writes results.json and results.md. Every measurement on this page comes from those files.
